The internship

A high schooler in a genomics institute

In 2024, while I was still in high school, I interned at the J. Craig Venter Institute — a genomics research institute — on a project to classify phytoplankton from microscope imagery.

I went in wanting to study engineering with a focus on biomedical engineering, and I took the internship mostly to find out whether that instinct was right. The honest goal was to explore: to see what research actually looks like from the inside, and to find out whether my path led toward engineering or somewhere further into the overlap of technology and healthcare.

What I did not expect was how much of the work would turn out to be teaching a model to see. This page is an archive of that project and the notes I kept while doing it.

The project

Classifying diatoms, and watching for blooms

My project was the image classification of phytoplankton — specifically diatoms, and the monitoring of algal blooms. The work meant using imaging and computational techniques to analyze photographs of these organisms, then turning those images into data: identifying and cataloging different species of diatom, and counting them.

That cataloging matters more than it sounds like it should. Diatoms are a key component of phytoplankton, and phytoplankton are the base of nearly every aquatic food web — so knowing which species are present, and in what numbers, is a readout on the health of the whole system.

The second half of the work was tracking algal blooms, which can have serious environmental consequences for water quality and marine life. Getting there meant applying mathematical models and machine learning to improve how reliably blooms could be detected and understood — small contributions to a much larger body of environmental research and management.

  • Image classification

    Researching how classification methods work, then putting them to use on real microscopy images.

  • Model training

    Building a classifier in Roboflow to sort phytoplankton by type and quantify cell counts.

  • Species identification

    Learning to tell diatoms apart by shape and structure — the hardest part, and the most necessary.

  • Bloom monitoring

    Using models to help detect and track algal blooms before they affect water quality.

Field notes

The log I kept while the work was happening

These entries are reproduced as I wrote them at the time — present tense, week by week, unedited in substance. They are the most accurate record of what the internship actually felt like from the inside.

Week 1

May 2024

On Monday and Tuesday, my objectives were to research image classification and learn about phytoplankton. I delivered an explanation of image classification methods and a summary of phytoplankton. I'm proud of my understanding of image classification but realized I need to delve deeper into identifying different types of phytoplankton.

I'm developing a program using Roboflow to classify various types of phytoplankton and quantify their cell counts. This week's goal is to understand different types of phytoplankton better.

On Wednesday and Thursday, my objectives were to implement initial image classification models and begin training these models on phytoplankton images. I successfully set up the model and started the training process, which I'm proud of.

This project will benefit my understanding of machine learning and image processing, and help JCVI by improving phytoplankton monitoring methods.

I'm learning a lot from my mentor about image classification and project management, and our communication is excellent.

Jacob Archbold, another intern, is learning to draw buildings to scale and use a scale ruler. I like to think I'm a lot better >:)
